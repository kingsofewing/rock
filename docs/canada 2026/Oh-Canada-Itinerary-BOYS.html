Thu, Jun 25 — Home → Cornell, WI

~5 hr≈290 mi · I-90/94 🗺️ Route
Eat: Norske Nook in Osseo — award-winning Norwegian pie right off the highway. Get a slice (or a whole pie) for the road.
Dinner: Moonridge Brewing pizza in Cornell.
🏕️ Camp: Brunet Island State Park — an island campground on the Chippewa; canoe the quiet lagoons and hit the swim beach.

🦫 Wild Things — Chippewa River country

  • Beaver: the second-biggest rodent on Earth. Its orange front teeth are coated in iron (that's why they're orange) and never stop growing — if it didn't gnaw wood daily, its teeth would curl into its own skull. It eats bark and the soft layer underneath, not fish, and its lodge door is underwater so predators can't get in. Gross bonus: beavers mark territory with castoreum, a goo squeezed from sacs next to the butt — it smells like vanilla, and people used to put it in perfume and food flavoring.
  • River otter: can hold its breath ~8 minutes and close its nose and ears underwater. The whole family shares one bathroom spot on the bank called a "latrine" — they poop in the same place on purpose to leave messages for other otters.
  • Bald eagle: nests right on Brunet Island. Eagles build the biggest tree nest of any North American bird — up to 2 tons, reused for years. They're also thieves: they'll dive-bomb an osprey and make it drop its fish in mid-air.
  • Lake sturgeon: a living fossil swimming the Chippewa — armored in bony plates instead of scales, can live 100+ years and grow over 6 feet, and vacuums food off the river bottom with a tube-like mouth that has no teeth.

🧭 Did you know? — Chippewa Valley, WI

  • Wisconsin is the "Badger State" — named not for the animal but for 1800s lead miners who dug into hillsides like badgers (the real badger is a stocky, bad-tempered digger that can tunnel out of sight in under a minute).
  • Cornell has the world's only surviving "pulpwood stacker" — a 175-foot tower built in 1912 to pile logs for a paper mill.
  • Nearby Chippewa Falls is home of Leinenkugel's, the 7th-oldest brewery in the US (1867), and the birthplace of Seymour Cray, the "father of the supercomputer."

Fri, Jun 26 — Cornell → Ely, MN (through Duluth)

~4 hr≈210 mi 🗺️ Route
Stop: Duluth Canal Park — eat with a Lake Superior view and watch the giant Aerial Lift Bridge raise for ships. Bonus climb: Enger Tower.
Near camp: the International Wolf Center & North American Bear Center in Ely — real wolves and bears.
🏕️ Camp: Bear Head Lake State Park — swim and paddle a clear Northwoods lake ringed by pines.

🐺 Wild Things — Northwoods & Boundary Waters

  • Gray wolf: Ely sits at the edge of the lower-48's biggest wolf country. A wolf can smell prey from over 1.5 miles away and gulp ~20 pounds of meat in one sitting — that's where "wolfing it down" comes from. Gross bonus: parents can't carry groceries, so they swallow meat, trot back to the den, and vomit it up for the pups to eat. Their poop ("scat") is full of hair and crushed bone.
  • Moose: the largest deer alive — taller than a horse. It eats 40–60 pounds of plants a day and will dive completely underwater, closing its nostrils, to rip up plants from the lake bottom and hold its breath ~30 seconds. That flap of skin swinging under its throat is called a "bell."
  • Black bear: before winter it eats up to 20,000 calories a day (like 40 burgers). Then it dens for months without eating, drinking, peeing, or pooping — its body recycles its own pee, and a hard "fecal plug" seals its rear end shut the whole winter. Its nose is about 7× sharper than a bloodhound's.
  • Common loon (MN state bird): unlike most birds its bones are solid, not hollow, so it sinks and can dive 200 feet. Its legs are set so far back it can barely walk on land and needs a quarter-mile "runway" of flapping across the water to take off. It swallows fish whole — and swallows small pebbles to grind food up in its gut.

🧭 Did you know? — Lake Superior & the Northwoods

  • Lake Superior is the largest freshwater lake on Earth by surface area — it holds about 10% of all the planet's surface fresh water, stays near 40°F year-round, and is so big it makes its own fog and weather.
  • It's also the deepest Great Lake — 1,333 ft (406 m) straight down — and the third-largest lake on Earth by volume. There's enough water in it to cover all of North AND South America a foot deep.
  • Out in the lake sits Isle Royale, home to the longest-running wildlife study in the world (since 1958): wolves vs. moose. The moose swam ~15 miles to get there, and the first wolves walked over on a winter ice bridge.
  • Because it's so cold and deep, the lake preserves shipwrecks almost perfectly — sailors used to say "Superior never gives up her dead."
  • Duluth is the farthest-inland seaport in North America — ocean ships sail 2,300+ miles from the Atlantic to get there.

🎮 Spot it

Watch the water in Duluth for a "laker" — a freighter up to 1,000 feet long. Call it out first and you win the round.

Sat, Jun 27 — Ely, MN → Sioux Narrows, ON 🇨🇦

~5 hr≈230 mi + border 🗺️ Route
Photo stop: the giant Smokey Bear statue in International Falls before we cross.
Eat: Snackhouse in Sioux Narrows — burgers and fries.
🏕️ Camp: Sioux Narrows Provincial Park — right on Lake of the Woods: swim, fish for walleye, watch the sunset over the islands.

🐟 Wild Things — Lake of the Woods & the Canadian Shield

  • American white pelican: one of the biggest birds in North America, with a 9-foot wingspan and a throat pouch that holds 3 gallons of water. It doesn't dive — a whole line of pelicans herds fish into the shallows and scoops them up like nets. Gross bonus: a chick eats by shoving its entire head down the parent's throat to slurp out half-digested fish soup.
  • Walleye: the fish this whole region is obsessed with. Its eyes have a mirror layer (like a cat's) that glows and lets it hunt in the dark and in murky water — which is exactly why its name is "wall-eye."
  • Snapping turtle: too big to pull fully into its shell, so it defends itself by biting — a beak strong enough to take a finger. It eats almost anything, including dead stuff rotting on the lake bottom (nature's garbage crew).
  • Jack pine: the scrappy tree clinging to the bare Shield rock. Its cones are glued shut with resin and only pop open in a wildfire's heat — so the tree literally needs forest fires to make baby trees.

🧭 Did you know? — The Border & the Shield

  • Crossing into Ontario you roll onto the Canadian Shield — the largest patch of exposed ancient rock on Earth, covering about half of Canada. The bedrock is up to a few billion years old (the oldest, the Acasta Gneiss, is ~4 billion), scraped bare by glaciers so the soil is paper-thin.
  • Hidden in the Shield is the Sudbury Basin — a giant meteorite crater blasted out 1.85 billion years ago. It's the second-biggest impact crater on Earth, you can see it from space, and the blast threw rock all the way to Minnesota. Apollo astronauts even trained there to learn to spot space rocks.
  • Lake of the Woods has 14,522 islands — walk around all of them and you'd cover about 65,000 miles of shoreline, more than twice around the planet. Sioux Narrows sits right on it, and its rocky shores hide ancient rock paintings (pictographs) made by First Nations — some of the oldest in Canada.
  • International Falls is nicknamed the "Icebox of the Nation" for some of the coldest winter temps in the continental US.

🎮 Border bonus

First country flip of the trip. New game: start spotting Ontario plates and Canadian road signs in kilometres — quick, what's 100 km/h in mph? (~62)

Sun, Jun 28 — Sioux Narrows → Victoria Beach, MB 🇨🇦

~3.5 hr≈250 km · all in Canada 🗺️ Route
Stop: lunch in Kenora on the way — big dog-friendly patio at Yesterday's.
Arrive: the cottage! Stock up at the Victoria Beach general store. Treats: Le Gouter.

🐍 Wild Things — where the forest becomes prairie

  • Red-sided garter snake: a couple hours from the cottage, near Narcisse, is the biggest snake gathering on the planet — limestone pits where up to tens of thousands of garter snakes pile up to survive winter. Every spring the males swarm each female into a writhing "mating ball" of dozens of snakes. By the time you visit in summer they've slithered out across the marshes to hunt frogs and worms.
  • Richardson's ground squirrel: the "gophers" standing like little posts in the prairie fields. They sleep through winter AND through the hottest part of summer (that double nap is called estivation + hibernation), spending up to 9 months a year asleep underground.
  • Red-tailed hawk: watch the fence posts and sky over the open fields. It hunts by hovering, then drops on mice and gophers — and its eyesight is roughly 8× sharper than yours.

🧭 Did you know? — Victoria Beach & Lake Winnipeg

  • Out the window the rocky Canadian Shield flattens into wide-open prairie — the bottom of a giant Ice-Age lake (Lake Agassiz) that was once bigger than all the Great Lakes combined.
  • Victoria Beach is the only community in North America that bans private cars in July and August — you park outside and walk, bike, or cab in.
  • It sits on Lake Winnipeg, one of the ten largest freshwater lakes in the world — shallow, so it actually warms up enough to swim.

🎮 Landscape watch

Call the exact moment the rocky, tree-covered Shield turns into flat, treeless prairie. Whoever nails it gets shotgun next leg.

Mon Jun 29 – Thu Jul 2 — Cottage life, Victoria Beach 🇨🇦

0 hrno driving — base camp
Do: beach days, swimming, biking the car-free streets, and day trips — The Forks in Winnipeg (~1.5 hr) or Whiteshell/Pinawa hikes.
Treats: Le Gouter, Village Green Bakery, Salty's Drive-Inn.

🐸 Wild Things — lake, marsh & boreal forest

  • Great blue heron: stalks the shallows on stilt legs, then spears or snatches a fish and swallows it whole, headfirst — sometimes a fish is so big it gets stuck and you can watch the lump go down its neck. Gross bonus: herons blast their poop backward away from the nest in a white "whitewash," and cough up pellets of bones and scales they can't digest.
  • Wood frog: living in these woods is a frog with a superpower — in winter it lets two-thirds of its body freeze solid. Its heart stops, it stops breathing, and it pumps its blood full of natural antifreeze (sugar). In spring it thaws out and hops away, alive.
  • Common loon & muskrat: listen at dusk for the loon's wail across the lake. In the reeds, muskrats build dome huts of mud and cattails and can swim backward and chew underwater without flooding their mouths.
  • The rock itself: the pink-and-gray granite around the Whiteshell is billion-year-old Shield bedrock flecked with sparkly quartz and dark red garnet crystals — worth a close look on a bored afternoon.

🧭 Did you know? — Manitoba

  • Winnie-the-Pooh is named after Winnipeg. In 1914 a Canadian soldier, Harry Colebourn, bought a real black bear cub, named her "Winnie" after his hometown, and gave her to the London Zoo — where she inspired the stories.
  • The nearby Whiteshell has ancient Indigenous petroforms — rocks laid out into shapes of snakes, turtles, and people, some thousands of years old.
  • Manitoba is nicknamed the "Land of 100,000 Lakes" — even more than Minnesota claims.

🎮 Cottage challenge

Four days, no car. Goals: swim every day, find a chunk of garnet-flecked granite, and hear a loon call after dark.

Fri, Jul 3 — Victoria Beach → Williams, MN 🇺🇸

~4 hr≈250 km · back to the USA 🗺️ Route
Eat: Lake of the Woods Pizza Company in Baudette.
🏕️ Camp: Zippel Bay State Park — walk the white-sand beach and the stone jetty out into Lake of the Woods; scan for piping plovers.

🦅 Wild Things — Zippel Bay & Lake of the Woods

  • Piping plover: Zippel Bay's white-sand beach is one of only four places in the whole region this tiny endangered shorebird still nests. When a fox or gull gets near its eggs, the parent fakes a "broken wing," flopping away pretending to be hurt to lure the predator off — then flies away fine.
  • Sandhill crane: nests on the north side of the bay and stands over 4 feet tall. Gross bonus: cranes paint themselves — they smear mud and rusty iron from the soil onto their feathers to dye gray into camouflage-brown. Their bugling call carries 2.5 miles, and they'll happily eat other birds' eggs.
  • Also watch for: white pelicans, bald eagles, river otters, and — back in the woods — timber wolves and moose, all of which live around this park.

🧭 Did you know? — Warroad, Roseau & Baudette

  • Lake of the Woods — the giant lake you're camping on — touches three places at once (Minnesota, Ontario, and Manitoba). It's mostly shallow (~26 ft) but drops to 210 ft deep, and people call it "the other Great Lake."
  • Baudette is the "Walleye Capital of the World," guarded by Willie the Walleye, a 40-foot, 2½-ton fish statue.
  • Warroad is "Hockeytown USA" — a tiny town that's sent players to multiple Olympics — and Roseau is the birthplace of the snowmobile.
  • Just east is the Northwest Angle — the only piece of the lower-48 US north of the Canada line, and you can only drive to it through Canada.

Sat, Jul 4 — Williams → Two Harbors, MN

~4.5 hr≈250 mi · 🎆 July 4th 🗺️ Route
Stop: Bemidji — photo with the giant Paul Bunyan & Babe the Blue Ox.
Eat: Black Woods Grill or Lake Superior Burger Co. Sweet: Betty's Pies.
🏕️ Camp: Gooseberry Falls State Park — walk the Upper, Middle & Lower Falls and hunt agates on the Lake Superior shore.

🦅 Wild Things — North Shore cliffs

  • Peregrine falcon: nests on the North Shore's lava cliffs and is the fastest animal alive — it folds its wings and dives ("stoops") at over 200 mph, then punches its prey out of the air. To keep that 200-mph wind from blowing its lungs apart, it has tiny bony cones inside its nostrils that slow the air down — the same idea engineers later copied for jet engines.
  • Lake Superior agate: Minnesota's state gem — banded red-and-orange stones that formed as quartz filled gas bubbles inside the cooling lava a billion years ago, then got tumbled smooth by glaciers. Finding one is a real trophy. Hunt the gravel beach at Gooseberry.
  • Black bear: common all along the North Shore. Gross bonus: a bear's poop changes color with the season — dark from meat and bugs in spring, then purple and seedy from gorging on berries in late summer.

🧭 Did you know? — Bemidji & the North Shore

  • Bemidji calls itself the "first city on the Mississippi River" — here the giant river is just a shallow stream you could wade across.
  • The North Shore cliffs are ~1.1-billion-year-old lava flows from a time when North America almost ripped in half (the Midcontinent Rift).
  • Bemidji built the Paul Bunyan & Babe statues back in 1937.

🎮 Agate hunt

Gooseberry Falls drops over ancient lava ledges. While you're there, hunt the shoreline — first to find a real banded agate wins.

Sun, Jul 5 — Two Harbors → Brule, WI

~1.5 hr≈75 mi · short day! 🗺️ Route
Stop: Split Rock Lighthouse — one of the most photographed spots on Lake Superior, perched on a 130-foot cliff.
Eat: Kro Bar in Brule, or a Culver's/A&W run in Superior.
🏕️ Camp: Brule River State Forest — the ice-cold, spring-fed "River of Presidents"; great for wading and rock-flipping.

🐟 Wild Things — the Brule River

  • Brook trout: the Brule is spring-fed and ice-cold, which is exactly what trout need — they can only survive in clean, cold, oxygen-rich water, so a healthy trout stream is proof the water is pure. They eat drifting bugs and will leap clear out of the water to grab a fly.
  • Leech: yes, the northwoods lakes have them. Gross bonus: a leech has three jaws with ~100 tiny teeth, and its spit contains a chemical that stops blood from clotting so it can keep feeding — doctors still use real leeches in surgery today. (Cold-blooded fact: most lake leeches actually eat snails and bugs, not people.)
  • Crayfish: the mini-lobsters under the rocks. They can drop a claw to escape a predator and grow it back, and they swim backward by flipping their tail. Flip a rock in the shallows and you'll find one.

🧭 Did you know? — Split Rock & the Brule

  • Split Rock Lighthouse exists because a single furious storm in November 1905 wrecked or damaged 29 ships on Lake Superior in one night.
  • The most famous wreck of all, the Edmund Fitzgerald, sank in a Lake Superior storm on Nov 10, 1975 with all 29 crew — its last radio words were "We are holding our own." A famous song was written about it, and an estimated 550 shipwrecks rest on the lake's bottom.
  • The spring-fed Brule drops 420 feet on its way to Lake Superior, making tons of rapids — and it's the "River of Presidents," where five US presidents fished.
  • Watch the Split Rock cliffs for nesting peregrine falcons and ravens riding the wind off the lake.

🎮 Free time

Shortest drive of the trip = most goofing-off time. Lighthouse photo, skip rocks, hunt more agates, flip rocks for crayfish.

Mon, Jul 6 — Brule → Wisconsin Dells

~4 hr≈240 mi south 🗺️ Route
Eat: a second Norske Nook pie hit in Osseo is fair game.
Afternoon: Wisconsin Dells — the "Waterpark Capital of the World." Dinner: Grateful Shed food hall or Brat House Grill.
🏕️ Camp: Mirror Lake State Park — calm, glassy water under sandstone bluffs; swim or paddle before the Dells.

🦤 Wild Things — Baraboo & the Dells gorge

  • Cranes: nearby Baraboo has the International Crane Foundation — the only place on Earth you can see all 15 species of crane. The whooping crane, the tallest bird in North America, dropped to about 15 birds left alive in 1941, and this place helped bring it back from the edge.
  • The gorge: the Dells is a 5-mile canyon of 500-million-year-old sandstone — packed sand from an ancient sea — that got blasted open ~15,000 years ago when a glacier-dammed lake burst and a wall of meltwater tore through in days.
  • Little brown bat: Wisconsin's caves and bluffs are full of them. One bat eats up to 1,000 mosquito-sized bugs an hour at night, finding them in total darkness by echolocation — screaming and listening for the echo.

🧭 Did you know? — Wisconsin Dells & Baraboo

  • The land along the Dells is sacred to the Ho-Chunk Nation, whose ancient burial mounds — many shaped like animals — remain nearby.
  • Next-door Baraboo is the birthplace of the Ringling Brothers Circus (1884) — now a museum with 260+ antique circus wagons.
  • The sandstone here is soft enough to carve with a fingernail, which is why the river cut it into such crazy cliffs and pillars.

Tue, Jul 7 — Dells → Home

~2.5 hr≈170 mi · last leg 🗺️ Route
Eat: Madison for a real lunch, or grab-and-go.

🌾 Wild Things — prairie & farm country home

  • Sandhill crane: scan the cut farm fields of southern Wisconsin — flocks of these tall gray cranes feed here, and they're one of the oldest bird types on Earth, basically unchanged for millions of years.
  • Turkey vulture: the dark V-winged bird rocking side to side over the highway. It finds dead animals by smell from a mile up, and pulls two of the grossest defense moves around: it pees down its own legs to cool off, and when threatened it projectile-vomits a foul, half-digested slurry at the attacker.
  • Home stretch: as the prairie returns, you're crossing the flat bed of an ancient glacial lake again — the same kind of landscape that started flattening out back in Manitoba.

🧭 Did you know? — Madison & home

  • Wisconsin's capital, Madison, is built on a narrow isthmus — a skinny strip of land squeezed between two lakes.
  • The State Capitol dome is the only granite dome in the US, and by law no downtown building may be taller than it.
  • Cross back into Illinois — the "Prairie State" — and you're almost home.

  • Spot-it scorecard: 1 pt for a chipmunk/squirrel, 2 for a deer, 3 for a bald eagle or great blue heron, 5 for a loon, beaver, otter, or pelican, 10 for a moose, bear, wolf, fox, or sandhill crane. Keep a running tally per person.
