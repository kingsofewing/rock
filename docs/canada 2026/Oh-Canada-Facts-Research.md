## 1. Lake Superior

### Geography
- **Largest freshwater lake in the world by surface area** — about 31,700 sq mi (82,100 sq km). That's roughly the size of South Carolina, or all the other four Great Lakes would *not* quite fill it by area.
- **Deepest of the Great Lakes** — max depth 1,333 ft (406 m); average depth 483 ft (147 m).
- **Third-largest lake in the world by volume** (after Lake Baikal and Lake Tanganyika): ~2,900 cubic miles (12,100 cubic km) of water.
- Holds about **3 quadrillion gallons** — roughly **10% of all the fresh surface water on Earth**. Spread evenly, it could cover all of North and South America in about a foot of water.
- ~350 mi long (east–west) and up to ~160 mi wide (north–south). Shoreline (with islands) stretches ~2,726 mi (4,387 km).
- **Cold, always.** Average year-round water temperature is about 40°F (4.4°C); even in summer the surface rarely tops 55°F (13°C), though shallow protected bays (e.g., Apostle Islands) can reach the 70s on calm sunny days.

### Animals & Wildlife
- **Lake trout** are the signature native fish — Superior holds the **only naturally self-sustaining lake trout population left in the Great Lakes**, in four distinct forms: lean, siscowet, humper, and redfin. The fatty **siscowet** thrives in the deep, frigid offshore water.
- **Lake whitefish** are a major catch — over a million pounds harvested yearly.
- **Lake sturgeon** are the biggest fish in the lake — they can live up to **100 years**.
- About **88 fish species total, 34 of them native**.
- **Isle Royale** (the largest island in Lake Superior, and Michigan's only national park) hosts the **longest continuous predator–prey study in the world** — wolves vs. moose, running since **1958**. Moose are thought to have first reached the island around 1900 by swimming ~15 miles of open water; wolves arrived on an ice bridge around 1949.

### History
- **The Edmund Fitzgerald** — a 729-ft ore freighter that sank in a furious storm on **Nov 10, 1975**, near Whitefish Bay. All **29 crew** were lost, with no distress call and no survivors. Her last radio message: *"We are holding our own."* Gordon Lightfoot's 1976 ballad made her the most famous shipwreck of the Great Lakes.
- An estimated **550 shipwrecks** rest on Superior's bottom.
- "**Superior never gives up her dead**" — because the water stays so cold, the bacteria that would normally cause a drowned body to bloat and resurface barely function, so bodies tend to stay below.

### Trivia / Did You Know
- The Ojibwe name is **Gitche Gumee** (roughly "big water" / "big sea"). Henry Wadsworth Longfellow used it in his 1855 poem *The Song of Hiawatha* — "the shining Big-Sea-Water."
- Superior is so big it essentially **makes its own weather**, including fog banks and sudden storms.
- It is the **highest** of the Great Lakes in elevation (~600 ft above sea level) and feeds all the others downstream.

## 2. The Canadian Shield

### Geography & Geology
- A vast horseshoe of ancient rock wrapping around Hudson Bay, covering about **8 million sq km (~3 million sq mi)** — roughly **half of Canada's land area**, plus extensions into the northern U.S. and Greenland.
- The **largest area of exposed Precambrian rock on Earth** — the literal old bones of the continent.
- Some of the **oldest rock on the planet**: the **Acasta Gneiss** (Northwest Territories) is about **4.03 billion years old**, and greenstone beds on the east shore of Hudson Bay have been dated even older (~4.3 billion years, though that figure is debated).
- The landscape we see — bare rock, **thin soil, and thousands upon thousands of lakes** — was **scraped clean by the ice-age glaciers**, which gouged out the lake basins and stripped the soil. Soils that remain are thin, acidic, and slow to rebuild.
- Mostly a **rolling upland** rather than mountains — elevations rarely exceed 500–700 m across the southern Shield.
- Blanketed by **boreal forest** (taiga): spruce, pine, fir, and birch.

### Animals & Wildlife
- Big mammals: **moose, woodland caribou, white-tailed deer, black bear, gray wolf, lynx, snowshoe hare, fisher, and marten**.
- Wetland and water animals: **beaver, muskrat, and mink** thrive in the endless ponds, rivers, and lakes.
- Birds: each spring the water draws **hundreds of thousands of ducks, loons, geese, and swans**. Also **boreal and great horned owls, evening grosbeaks, blue jays**, and the white-throated sparrow — the songbird most associated with this country.
- Fish of Shield lakes: **lake trout, northern pike, walleye, and burbot**.

### History & Resources
- One of the **richest mining regions on Earth** — a primary global source of **nickel, gold, copper, iron, silver, and uranium**. Its rivers also power large **hydroelectric** developments.
- The Shield has been home to Indigenous peoples for thousands of years; its lakes and rivers became the **highways of the fur trade**.

### Trivia / Did You Know
- The **Sudbury Basin** is a **1.85-billion-year-old meteorite (or comet) crater** — a bolide ~10–15 km wide slammed in at up to 30 km/s. It's the **second-largest known impact crater on Earth** (after Vredefort in South Africa), a 30-by-60 km scar **visible from space**. The impact created the metal riches that made Sudbury one of the world's great mining towns.
- Debris from that impact was hurled over 800 km — bits of it have been found as far away as **Minnesota**.
- **Apollo astronauts trained at Sudbury** to learn to recognize impact rocks — and Sudbury is the **only Canadian city referenced by an astronaut on the surface of the Moon** (Apollo 16).

## 3. Lake of the Woods

### Geography
- Straddles **three jurisdictions**: Minnesota (U.S.), Ontario, and Manitoba (Canada).
- **About 14,522 islands** — and so much crinkled coastline that the shoreline totals **~25,000 miles**, or **~65,000 miles** if you trace every island. (For comparison, Earth's circumference is ~24,900 miles — so the island-inclusive shoreline could wrap the planet more than twice.)
- ~85 mi long and ~56 mi at its widest.
- **Average depth just ~26 ft, but max depth ~210 ft.** The basin was carved by glaciers.
- The **Northwest Angle** ("the Angle") is a thumb of Minnesota poking north into the lake — **the northernmost point of the contiguous United States**, and the only part of the lower 48 you can reach by land *only* by driving through Canada (or crossing the lake).

### Animals & Wildlife
- **The "Walleye Capital of the World"** — walleye by the millions; the lake yields hundreds of thousands of pounds a year.
- Also loaded with **muskie** (one of the legendary "big three" Canadian Shield muskie lakes), **northern pike, sauger, yellow perch, crappie, whitefish, tullibee, burbot,** and **lake sturgeon** (rebounding strongly on the lake and the Rainy River).
- **American white pelicans** nest on the lake's islands — big white birds with 9-foot wingspans, surprisingly far north. **Black terns, ring-billed and herring gulls** also nest in the Sand Spit Archipelago; bald eagles patrol the shorelines.

### History
- A key artery of the **fur trade** — well into the 1800s, voyageurs paddled the route east from the Red River settlement through Lake of the Woods toward the Great Lakes and Montreal. The Hudson's Bay Company and North West Company both worked the region (they merged in 1821).
- The lake created one of North America's **strangest borders.** The 1783 treaty drew the line to "the northwesternmost point" of Lake of the Woods — but the map everyone used was wrong, and the lake had no clear northwest point. Surveyor **Johann Ludwig Tiarks** pinned it down in the 1820s at today's **Angle Inlet**, and the odd jog was locked in by the **Webster–Ashburton Treaty (1842)** — which is why the Northwest Angle exists.

### Trivia / Did You Know
- The name is simply the English of the French **"Lac des Bois"** — "lake of the woods."
- The Lake of the Woods area holds **hundreds of First Nations pictograph sites** painted on the Shield rock. At **Mud Portage**, petroglyphs found buried in archaeological layers have been dated to **more than 5,000 years ago — among the oldest in Canada.**
- It's sometimes called **"the other Great Lake"** for its sheer size and island count.
